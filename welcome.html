  1. Give it a meeting. Pick a room name at gifos.app/meet. A majority-rules meeting (an open meeting) is free at any size, and the room can vote anyone off the Stage, bots too.   2. Bring the bot. Three ways, today:
    • The agent SDK npm install @gifos-app/agent (Node 20+, with Chrome or Edge on the computer), or from Python pip install gifos-agent (it drives the same Node seat). Your code hears every line said or typed and answers in chat, or out loud with a voice. Bring your own model and your own facts: 
      import { join } from '@gifos-app/agent';

const meet = await join('https://gifos.app/meet#room=YOUR-ROOM', { name: "Ava (Acme's guide)" });

Chapter 1

Meet: one link, no sign-up

Type a room name, share the link, and everyone's in, on a computer or a phone, right in the browser.

What it solves

  • A sign-up, a download or a phone app before you can talk.
  • Seat caps of 100 or 300.
  • Big calls where only a few people can ever speak.
  • Losing the call the moment you need your screen back.

A majority-rules meeting (an open meeting) belongs to nobody: if someone misbehaves, the room votes them off the Stage or out of the meeting. You choose how you appear: max blur, min blur or clear.

The meeting grows with the crowd. The first people sit on the Stage; as more arrive they fill rows, and each row talks among itself and hears the Stage. Rows fill a Stadium, from ten people to thousands on the same link. There are live captions, a transcript you can download, and a chat that is gone when everyone leaves. Tap Float and the meeting follows you in a small window.

Chapter 2

Hosted meetings: a meeting you control

The host holds a host password, and faces stay blurred until the host sets a room password.

What it solves

  • Strangers switching their cameras on in your meeting.
  • Privacy that only holds if every guest's software behaves.
  • Audiences who must show their faces just to watch the speaker.

The host can mute or blur anyone for everyone, turn chat off, allow screen share, run a waiting room and remove people. The room password is the key to clear video, and the blur is applied by the people watching, not only by the sender, so even a tampered camera feed still shows up blurred.

With Clear Stage on a paid plan, the host lets viewers tap to see the Stage clear while the audience stays private. Hosted meetings are free for up to 5 people.

Chapter 4

Embed it on any website

One line puts a live GifOS meeting or broadcast on your page. The welcome meeting at the top of this page is one.

What it solves

  • A vendor's player and a contract to put live video on your site.
  • Viewers who leave your site to watch.

Viewers can unmute, chat, react, go full screen, or float the player while they keep reading. They never turn on a camera or a microphone. Hosted rooms stay blurred in the player too, unless the host allows a clear Stage. The player plays while a host is in charge of the room and shows a short card the rest of the time.

The script tag (works on most sites). Put your room's name in place of ROOM:

<script src="https://gifos.app/embed.js" data-room="ROOM"></script>

The plain iframe (for sites that remove scripts):

<iframe src="https://gifos.app/embed#room=ROOM" style="width:100%;aspect-ratio:16/9;border:0" allow="autoplay; fullscreen; picture-in-picture" allowfullscreen title="GifOS Broadcast"></iframe>

For a hosted meeting, add its verifier (the v= in its link, gifos.app/cast#room=ROOM&v=VERIFIER) as data-verifier, or &v=VERIFIER in the iframe.

Choose the controls

The player starts as the picture, the live badge, unmute, and at the bottom right the buttons for chat, transcript, reactions, info, float and full screen beside a settings gear. The bar fades after a few seconds and comes back on any pointer, tap or key. When you set nothing, the player offers volume,audioonly,fullscreen,pip,chat,transcript,reactions,names,watch. The info button always shows.

volume: a volume slider in the gear.

mute: a mute switch in the gear.

captions: a captions switch in the gear. It appears once a line has arrived. Captions stay off until the viewer turns them on.

audioonly: an audio-only switch in the gear: the viewer's screen stops drawing the pictures and the sound goes on.

tabs: the share tabs, when the room shares something besides the Stage.

inset: show, hide or move the Stage picture while a share fills the player.

fullscreen: the full screen button.

pip: the float button (picture in picture), when the browser supports it.

chat: the chat button, while the host leaves chat on. A viewer gives a name the first time they post.

transcript: the transcript button: the lines the room's speakers caption, a Download (plain text, or a zip with screenshots), and an Auto screenshots box that starts off.

reactions: the reactions button, while the host lets guests react.

names: a Show names switch in the gear, on at first: the viewer can hide the name bars drawn on the Stage picture. It is kept in that viewer's browser.

watch: a "View on gifos.app" link in the info panel, opening the room on GifOS in a new tab.

all turns every control on. none leaves only the info button.

On the script tag, add data-controls="tabs,inset,captions,fullscreen,watch". On the iframe, add it to the hash: #room=ROOM&controls=tabs,inset. An iframe you write yourself keeps allow="autoplay; fullscreen; picture-in-picture" and allowfullscreen, or the full screen and float buttons do not show. The password prompt, the not-hosted notice and the info button always show.

Chapter 5

GifOS TV: a channel from a Raspberry Pi

A live broadcast that never stops: a hundred public-domain films, about seven hours of them, back to back, sent from a Raspberry Pi at home. This player is the same one line of code.

What it solves

  • A 24-hour channel needs a studio and servers.
  • There's no way to find live shows worth watching.
  • Hosts have no direct way to be supported.

The picture starts blurred: GifOS blurs video for viewers unless the room has a password. This broadcast's host has a plan with Clear Stage, so tap the picture to see the Stage clearly.

How we did it
  1. A small computer at home. A Raspberry Pi 4 (4 cores, 4 GB of memory) that also runs other home jobs. GifOS TV uses about a quarter of its processor and about a gigabyte of its memory.
  2. An ordinary GifOS seat. The Pi opens gifos.app in a headless Chromium browser, driven by a short Node.js script. It is a normal meeting seat with no camera and no microphone, named GifOS TV. Nothing about it is special to the servers: a bot follows the same rules as a person.
  3. A hosted broadcast. The seat is the host of a private broadcast room called gifos-tv. It signs in with the room's host password, and it carries a GifOS pass with no people limit, so the room takes as many viewers as come.
  4. The films. A hundred short films from Wikimedia Commons and the Internet Archive: cartoons, early animation, newsreels, music films, space and nature. We kept a film only when its own file page lists it as public domain or CC0, and only when it has sound all the way through (no long silences that would sound like a broken player). The full list with sources and credits is below.
  5. Prepared once, elsewhere. Another computer converted every film to one light format (640×360 pictures, VP8 video and Opus sound in WebM), evened out the loudness, and joined the films back to back into reels of about forty minutes. The Pi only keeps the reels, in a folder with a size cap, so it never fills its card.
  6. GifOS's own Rebroadcast button. Every broadcast host has it: More, Rebroadcast, Choose a video. The script presses it with the next reel, and when a reel ends it starts the next one, forever. Viewers see the Prerecorded badge.
  7. Peer to peer. GifOS meetings run between the people in them, not on our servers. Viewers pass the Stage along to one another, so a Pi on a home connection does not send a copy to everyone watching.
  8. Always on. The script runs as a service that restarts on failure. A watchdog restarts it if it stops reporting, and it rejoins on its own when GifOS ships a new release.
  9. On this page. The player above is this snippet:
<script src="https://gifos.app/embed.js" data-room="gifos-tv" data-verifier="cc51c5e4f4418c66aa52a486" data-title="GifOS TV"></script>

Chapter 6

Why it costs so little: peer to peer

The people in the meeting carry it. Each browser sends, receives and passes video along for its neighbours; our servers only introduce people.

What it solves

  • Video bills that grow with every viewer.
  • Media servers that can see your content.
  • Free tiers that vanish once the company has to pay for its servers.

Meetings are end-to-end encrypted. Voice and chat get through corporate and school firewalls on the free plan, still end-to-end encrypted; a paid plan connects video across up to 3, 6 or 20 firewalled networks, still end-to-end encrypted. A bigger meeting doesn't mean a bigger video bill for us, which is why majority-rules meetings are free, forever.

Chapter 7

Plans: no accounts, ever

Your plan lives on your devices. To use it on another one, copy it over with a QR code.

What it solves

  • Subscriptions tied to an email and a password.
  • Losing your plan when you change phones.
  • Paying per seat for everyone in the meeting.

Paid plans add bigger hosted meetings, connect video across up to 3, 6 or 20 firewalled networks (still end-to-end encrypted), and add Clear Stage. They start with a one-dollar four-hour pass; hosted meetings of up to 20 people are $2 a month, and with no people limit, $5 a month. The best plan present sizes the whole meeting: if one person has a plan, everyone in that meeting gets the bigger room.

Chapter 8

The OS: every app is a GIF

Under the meetings, GifOS is a whole computer in your browser. Every icon on the Home Screen is a GIF file, and every app is a signed GIF with a tiny filesystem inside it.

What it solves

  • Apps that need installing and updating.
  • Apps that phone home with your data.
  • Software that only runs where the vendor put it.

Open an app and it runs, sandboxed. By default an app can't reach the network at all; if it needs to, it asks, and you see exactly which sites. Apps run inside meetings too: start a game, and everyone in the meeting plays together.

Chapter 9

Steal this app, and mod it with your AI

The idea GifOS started with. When someone runs an app with you, in a meeting or from a link, you can press Steal and get your own working copy.

What it solves

  • You see an app you like in someone else's meeting, and there's no way to get it.
  • You can use software, but only its maker can change it.
  • Changing an app means a developer setup, an app store and a review.

You choose what comes with it: the app alone, the app as it was when you joined, or everything in it right now, like a game in progress. Everything means everything the room can see; each person's private app data never leaves their own browser. Your copy lands in your Stolen Apps folder.

Then make it yours: download it as one GIF and hand it to your own AI, one that can run code. The GifOS guide tells the AI how to unpack the app, change it and pack it back up. Drop the new GIF on your Home Screen and it runs. A mod is a new work, so it is marked Unsigned. To share it back, run it in a meeting, where anyone can steal it from you. Here, that's not rude. It's the whole point.

Chapter 10

The App Store: authors keep 97%

More than two hundred apps today: games, tools, creative apps and learning apps, many of them great open-source programs ported to GifOS with their authors credited.

What it solves

  • App stores that take 15 to 30 percent.
  • Payouts that come weeks later, through a middleman.
  • Buyers who need a card on file.

When an app sells, its author is paid directly, in USDC on Base, and GifOS keeps 3%. No account for the buyer, and no waiting for a payout.

Chapter 11

Providers: on-device AI for every app

Some apps give your computer a new power. Install an offline AI model, like Gemma, Whisper for captions, or a voice, and every app that uses AI answers from your own device.

What it solves

  • AI that sends what you type to a cloud.
  • Every app needing its own API key.

A Provider can't reach the network at all: GifOS refuses it. So the model can't send what you ask it anywhere.

Chapter 12

AI agents: the SDK and the MCP server

AI agents join a GifOS meeting the same way people do. The bots that host the welcome meeting are two of them.

What it solves

  • AI assistants can't join a meeting as themselves.
  • Bot platforms need back doors and special keys.
  • Every meeting product needs its own bot integration.

A bot plays by the same rules as everyone else: it wears a bot badge, it can't skip the line, and it can't see more than a person could. Build one with the agent SDK (npm install @gifos-app/agent), or give an AI assistant the MCP server so it can join a meeting and take part.

ComingThe Identity folder

A new kind of GIF, the ID GIF: a single fact about you, like "over eighteen", signed by an authority that checked your ID. When an app asks, you choose which ones to give it, and nothing else.

What it solves

  • Handing your whole ID to every site that wants to know your age.
  • Identity data piling up on other people's servers.
